Advanced Multimodal Frameworks for Sentiment-Aware Financial Forecasting: Methodologies for Noise Quantification and Cross-Market Analysis
1. Introduction: The Evolution of Sentiment in Financial Microstructure
The intersection of computational linguistics and quantitative finance has precipitated a fundamental paradigm shift in how market microstructure is understood and modeled. Historically, the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) served as the bedrock of financial theory, positing that asset prices instantaneously and fully reflect all available information, thereby rendering the prediction of price movements based on public data impossible. However, the advent of the digital age and the proliferation of high-frequency social media discourse have provided empirical evidence supporting the Adaptive Markets Hypothesis (AMH), which suggests that market efficiency is not a static condition but a dynamic evolutionary process influenced by behavioral biases, institutional friction, and the speed of information propagation.
This research report serves as a comprehensive foundational document for the graduation project titled "Sentiment-Driven Stock Trend Prediction" (Çulban & Bayraktar, 2026), aiming to significantly expand the scope and rigor of the initial proposal. The core premise of the proposed study—that integrating sentiment analysis with deep learning can enhance stock trend prediction—is sound. However, this report argues that the inclusion of raw sentiment scores alone is insufficient for state-of-the-art forecasting in modern high-frequency environments. Retail investor sentiment, particularly on platforms like X (formerly Twitter) and StockTwits, often exhibits "noise trader" characteristics—systematic irrationality, herding, and overreaction—that can be inversely correlated with future returns. Therefore, to truly enhance predictive accuracy, the proposed system must not only measure sentiment but also quantify its reliability.
The digitalization of financial discourse has created a "noisy" environment where the volume of data often exceeds its informational content. "Noise traders"—agents who trade on sentiment, hype, or erroneous beliefs rather than fundamental valuations—introduce a systematic risk factor into the market that traditional asset pricing models fail to capture. This report details the integration of Natural Language Processing (NLP) with quantitative finance, specifically focusing on the comparative dynamics between an emerging market (Borsa Istanbul - BIST 100) and a developed market (NASDAQ). It provides an exhaustive catalog of ready-to-use datasets, defines mathematical frameworks for measuring "noise" and "herding," and outlines a hybrid Deep Learning architecture (LSTM/GRU) capable of ingesting multimodal data streams.
By treating social media advice not merely as a directional signal but potentially as a contra-indicator under specific "high-noise" conditions, the improved methodology aims to capture the alpha generated by market inefficiencies driven by behavioral biases. This document identifies specific datasets, such as the rsmctn/bist-dp-lstm-trading-turkish_financial_news corpus for Turkey and the FNSPID for the US, and proposes a dual-branch neural architecture to fuse asynchronous textual data with synchronous financial time series. The ultimate objective is to transition the project from a standard sentiment analysis application to a sophisticated behavioral finance instrument capable of distinguishing between the "wisdom of the crowd" and the "madness of the mob."
2. Theoretical Foundation: Sentiment, Noise, and Limits to Arbitrage
To construct a robust predictive model, it is essential to understand the theoretical mechanisms that link unstructured text to numerical price movements. The fundamental premise of this research is that financial markets are not frictionless aggregators of fundamental information but are subject to "Noise Trader Risk," as postulated by De Long et al. (1990).1
2.1 The Noise Trader Theory and Systematic Risk
Traditional finance theory assumes that rational arbitrageurs will immediately correct any mispricing caused by irrational investors. If a stock is undervalued due to unjustified pessimism, rational traders will buy it until the price reflects its fundamental value. However, in reality, arbitrage is limited by risk and capital constraints. "Noise traders"—often retail investors acting on social media "hype" or panic rather than fundamental valuation—can create persistent price deviations.
The De Long et al. model (DSSW model) demonstrates that noise traders can create a systematic risk factor that cannot be diversified away. If noise traders are bullish, they drive prices up. Rational arbitrageurs, fearing that the noise traders might become even more bullish tomorrow (pushing prices further away from fundamentals), may be reluctant to bet against the trend. This "resale price risk" allows sentiment-driven mispricing to persist.1
●	Implications for Prediction: This theory suggests that sentiment is not just "random error" but a distinct, priceable factor. A predictive model must therefore identify the regime of the market. Is the market currently driven by fundamentals (where sentiment is a secondary correction) or by noise (where sentiment is the primary driver)?
●	Sentiment-Price Feedback Loops: High sentiment can drive prices up, which in turn fuels further bullish sentiment, creating a bubble. A predictive model trained only on "positive sentiment = price increase" will fail disastrously at the peak of such a bubble when the "smart money" begins to exit. The model must detect when sentiment has decoupled from fundamentals.3
2.2 The "Smart Money" vs. "Dumb Money" Dichotomy
A critical enhancement to the project's methodology is the differentiation between "Smart Money" (institutional, informed) and "Dumb Money" (retail, emotional). This distinction is vital for quantifying the reliability of social media signals.
●	Smart Money: Typically acts at the end of the trading day or in response to structural liquidity shifts. Institutional investors have access to superior information and execution algorithms. They are often contrarian at sentiment extremes, selling into retail euphoria and buying into retail panic.4
●	Dumb Money: Often active at the market open (reacting to overnight news and social media) and highly correlated with social media volume. Retail investors are prone to herding and overreaction. High levels of "Dumb Money" activity (e.g., high retail order imbalance) can be a signal of overheating.6
●	The "Inverse Cramer" Effect: Popularized in financial memes but grounded in behavioral theory, this phenomenon suggests that mainstream retail advice (represented by figures like Jim Cramer or trending tickers on Reddit) is often a counter-indicator. When retail sentiment reaches fever pitch, it often signals a local top. A sophisticated model should test the hypothesis that extreme retail bullishness predicts negative returns.8
2.3 Cross-Market Dynamics: BIST 100 vs. NASDAQ
The comparative nature of the project provides a unique opportunity to test the Adaptive Market Hypothesis (AMH) across different market structures.
●	Borsa Istanbul (BIST 100): Characterized as an emerging market with higher volatility and higher retail participation rates relative to total volume. Studies suggest that herding behavior is more pronounced in emerging markets during periods of stress.10 The impact of a sentiment shock might be more immediate and severe due to lower liquidity depth compared to the US. The "noise" component in BIST might be higher, making sentiment quantification more critical but potentially more predictive if noise can be filtered.
●	NASDAQ: A highly efficient, deep market where algorithmic trading dominates. Here, social media sentiment might act as a high-frequency signal (minutes/hours) rather than a daily trend driver, or it might be rapidly arbitraged away by HFT firms. The "Signal-to-Noise" ratio in NASDAQ tweets is likely different, with a higher volume of bot traffic and algorithmic noise.12
2.4 Information Asymmetry and the Role of Unstructured Data
The efficient market hypothesis relies on the assumption that information is costless and universally available. However, "alternative data," such as social media sentiment, represents a form of information that is costly to process (requiring NLP pipelines).
●	Processing Cost: The "cost" here is computational. Entities that can process millions of tweets in real-time gain an informational advantage.
●	Unstructured Nature: Unlike earnings reports (structured numbers), tweets are unstructured. Their "value" is probabilistic. A tweet's sentiment score is an estimate of market mood, not a definitive fact. This probabilistic nature requires models that can handle uncertainty, such as Bayesian networks or Deep Learning models with dropout/uncertainty estimation.13

3.3 Data Alignment and Preprocessing
A major methodological hurdle identified in the proposal is the "asynchronous nature of text streams." News flows continuously, while markets open and close.
The "Influence Window" Methodology:
Textual data must be mapped to the specific trading session it influences.29 We propose the following mapping algorithm for the project:
1.	Pre-Market Bucket: News/Tweets from Market Close ($T-1$, e.g., 17:00 NYT) to Market Open ($T$, e.g., 09:30 NYT).
○	Impact: Maps to the Open Price of Day $T$. This captures the "overnight" sentiment accumulation.
2.	Intraday Bucket: News/Tweets during Trading Hours ($T_{open}$ to $T_{close}$).
○	Impact: Maps to the Close Price or Intraday intervals (e.g., hourly bars).
3.	Post-Market Bucket: News/Tweets immediately after Market Close ($T_{close}$ to $T_{close} + 4h$).
○	Impact: Maps to the After-Hours Price or the next day's Open.
4.	Weekend/Holiday Bucket: Aggregate all text from Friday Close to Monday Open.
○	Impact: Maps to Monday's Gap Opening.
Normalization:
●	Financials: Normalize OHLCV data using Z-Score normalization (standardization) or Min-Max scaling to ensure the neural network converges efficiently.
●	Text:
○	English: Lowercasing, removing URLs, cashtag identification ($AAPL).
○	Turkish: Requires specific morphological normalization. Turkish is an agglutinative language where sentiment is often embedded in suffixes. The project must use libraries like Zemberek or TurkishNLP to lemmatize words correctly (e.g., extracting "düşüş" from "düşüşteydi").30
4. Methodologies for Quantifying "Unreliability" and Noise
This section addresses the core research query: How do we quantify the unreliability of social media advice if prediction fails? The project assumes that social media is often a "noisy" signal that can mislead prediction models if taken at face value. We introduce three specific methodologies to detect and correct for this noise, transforming "prediction failure" from a bug into a feature (a counter-trading signal).
4.1 Methodology 1: Herding Detection (The CSAD Metric)
Herding occurs when investors suppress their own private information and follow the market consensus. In social media, this manifests as an "echo chamber." When herding is high, sentiment becomes unreliable because it is self-reinforcing rather than informative.
4.1.1 Mathematical Formulation
We utilize the Cross-Sectional Absolute Deviation (CSAD) of returns as a proxy for herding.31 The Rational Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) predicts that as the absolute market return $|R_{m,t}|$ increases, the dispersion of individual stock returns $CSAD_t$ should also increase linearly (because stocks have different betas and react differently to news).

$$CSAD_t = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} |R_{i,t} - R_{m,t}|$$
Where $R_{i,t}$ is the return of stock $i$ and $R_{m,t}$ is the market return.
Herding Logic: If $|R_{m,t}|$ is very high (extreme market move) but $CSAD_t$ becomes low (dispersion decreases or increases less than linearly), it means all stocks are moving together regardless of their fundamentals. Investors are panic-buying or panic-selling everything. This is a state of high noise.
4.1.2 Integration into the Model
●	Calculate daily $CSAD_t$ for the BIST 100 and NASDAQ constituents.
●	Feature: Create a "Herding Flag" feature.
○	Model the relationship: $CSAD_t = \alpha + \gamma_1 |R_{m,t}| + \gamma_2 R_{m,t}^2 + \epsilon_t$.
○	If $\gamma_2$ is significantly negative, herding is present.33
●	Rule: When Herding is detected (non-linear decrease in dispersion), the reliability of the "Social Sentiment" feature in the prediction model should be down-weighted, or treated as a "Bubble/Crash" indicator rather than a fundamental signal.
4.2 Methodology 2: Sentiment-Price Divergence (The "Contrarian" Signal)
"Unreliability" often manifests as a divergence between what the crowd says (sentiment) and what the market does (price). Identifying this divergence turns a "failed" sentiment prediction into a profitable contrarian signal.
4.2.1 The Divergence Index ($DI$)
We define a Divergence Index to capture the conflict between sentiment momentum and price momentum.34

$$DI_t = \text{Norm}(SMA(Sentiment, 5)) - \text{Norm}(SMA(Price, 5))$$
Where $SMA$ is the Simple Moving Average over 5 days, and $Norm$ normalizes both series to a common scale (e.g., 0 to 1).
●	Interpretation:
○	$DI \approx 0$: Sentiment supports Price (Reliable). The trend is confirmed.
○	$DI > Threshold$ (Positive Divergence): Sentiment is Bullish, but Price is trending sideways or Down. This is a classic "Bull Trap" or "Retail Bagholding" scenario. The social media advice is unreliable. The "Smart Money" is likely selling into the retail buying.
○	$DI < -Threshold$ (Negative Divergence): Sentiment is Bearish, but Price is stable or rising (Wall of Worry). This indicates accumulation by informed traders despite retail fear.
4.2.2 The "Inverse Cramer" Logic
In highly retail-driven assets (like certain BIST 100 stocks or Meme Stocks in the US), extreme retail bullishness often precedes a decline.
●	Methodology: Train the model to identify "Peak Euphoria." If Sentiment Score > 0.8 (Extreme Bullish) AND Volume is Declining (Smart Money leaving), the model should predict a Down trend, effectively "shorting" the social media advice.8 This explicitly quantifies the unreliability: when the crowd is too sure, they are usually wrong.
4.3 Methodology 3: Smart Money Flow Index (SMFI)
To further separate "Signal" (Smart Money) from "Noise" (Dumb Money), we use intraday price dynamics.
●	Assumption: Retail traders (Noise) are most active at the Open (reacting to overnight tweets). Institutional traders (Smart Money) dominate the Close (reacting to liquidity and fundamentals).6
●	Calculation:

$$SMFI_t = SMFI_{t-1} - (P_{open} - P_{close})_{t}$$

(Simplified concept: If price pumps at open but dumps at close, Smart Money is selling).
●	Application:
○	If Twitter Sentiment is Bullish BUT Smart Money Flow is Bearish (Net Institutional Selling), the tweet sentiment is classified as Noise (Unreliable).
○	If Twitter Sentiment is Bullish AND Smart Money Flow is Bullish, the sentiment is Signal (Reliable).
5. Advanced Methodologies for Sentiment Quantification
The proposal mentions VADER and BERT. This section refines that choice and details the specific NLP architectures required for the dual-market approach.
5.1 Model Architecture for Sentiment Extraction
The choice of NLP model determines the quality of the sentiment features.
●	English (NASDAQ): FinBERT is the superior choice over VADER.
○	Why: FinBERT is a BERT model pre-trained on a massive corpus of financial texts (earnings calls, news). It understands that words like "liability," "risk," or "tightening" have specific financial contexts that generic models miss.13 VADER, being lexicon-based, often misclassifies financial jargon.
●	Turkish (BIST 100): BERTurk (uncased).38
○	Why: Turkish is agglutinative (words are formed by stringing suffixes). A simple bag-of-words model (like VADER) struggles with suffixes. BERTurk uses sub-word tokenization, making it far more robust to Turkish morphology.
○	Adaptation: The project must fine-tune BERTurk on the kap-turkish-financial-sentiment dataset to create a "Turkish FinBERT" proxy. This involves "Transfer Learning"—taking the language understanding of BERTurk and teaching it the specific vocabulary of the Borsa Istanbul (e.g., "KAP," "Temettü" (Dividend), "Bedelsiz" (Bonus Issue)).
5.2 Sentiment Scoring Metrics
Rather than a single "Polarity Score," the NLP pipeline should output a vector of sentiment dimensions:
1.	Polarity: Positive / Negative / Neutral.
2.	Subjectivity: Fact vs. Opinion (Important for distinguishing news from tweets).
3.	Intensity: Strong vs. Weak sentiment.
4.	Agreement: Variance of sentiment across all tweets for a given stock on a given day. High variance implies uncertainty/noise; low variance implies consensus (or herding).
6. Proposed Machine Learning Architecture
The proposal suggests LSTM/GRU. This is appropriate, but the architecture must be "Multimodal," specifically designed to fuse the heterogeneous data types (Numerical Time-Series + Textual Embeddings).
6.1 The Dual-Branch Network
Instead of simply concatenating sentiment scores to the price vector (Early Fusion), which often dilutes the signal, a Dual-Branch architecture is recommended:
1.	Branch A (Numerical): An LSTM layer processing the sequence of OHLCV and Technical Indicators (RSI, MACD, Herding Coefficient). This branch learns price momentum patterns.
2.	Branch B (Textual): A Transformer layer (like DistilBERT) or a separate LSTM layer processing the sequence of daily sentiment embeddings. This branch learns the "narrative" arc.
3.	Fusion Layer with Attention: An Attention Mechanism (e.g., Multi-Head Attention) sits on top of the two branches. It learns to "weigh" the importance of the text vs. price branch dynamically.13
○	Scenario A (Earnings Day): The model might learn to give 80% weight to the Sentiment Branch (News is driving price).
○	Scenario B (Quiet Day): The model might give 90% weight to the Market Data Branch (Technical trading dominates).
○	Benefit: This architecture inherently addresses the "unreliability" issue. If sentiment has been a poor predictor in the last $N$ days (high divergence), the Attention weights for the Sentiment Branch will decrease, effectively filtering out the noise.
6.2 Handling "Unreliability" via Loss Functions
To explicitly model the unreliability of social media:
●	Custom Loss Function: Use a loss function that penalizes errors more heavily during periods of high volatility or high sentiment-price divergence.
●	Regime-Switching: The model can be trained to output not just a price prediction, but also a "Confidence Score." If the Herding Coefficient or Divergence Index is high, the model's confidence should drop, alerting the user that the social media signal is unreliable.
7. Comparative Analysis Framework (Turkey vs. US)
A key contribution of this project is the cross-market comparison. The dynamics of "Sentiment Unreliability" are expected to differ significantly between Turkey and the US.
7.1 Hypothesis 1: Higher Sentiment Elasticity in BIST
●	Proposition: Due to lower liquidity and higher retail dominance, BIST 100 stocks will show a larger price reaction (elasticity) to a unit change in sentiment compared to NASDAQ stocks. In the US, HFT algorithms arbitrage away sentiment signals in milliseconds. In Turkey, the reaction might be slower and more prolonged.
●	Testing: Compare the coefficients (weights) of the sentiment variable in the prediction models for both markets.
7.2 Hypothesis 2: Greater "Noise" and Herding in BIST
●	Proposition: The "Herding Coefficient" (CSAD) will be statistically higher in the Turkish dataset, indicating that social media advice is less reliable (more prone to noise) in Turkey than in the US.
●	Implication: The "Contrarian" strategy (betting against the crowd) might be more profitable in the Turkish market context, whereas a "Momentum" strategy (following the crowd) might work better in specific US tech sectors during bull runs.

9. Conclusion
This research report establishes that the integration of sentiment analysis with stock prediction is not merely a data engineering task but a complex behavioral finance problem. By leveraging state-of-the-art datasets like FNSPID and localized resources for Borsa Istanbul, and by applying rigorous methodologies to quantify the "noise" in social media (CSAD, Divergence Index, SMFI), the proposed graduation project can transcend simple trend forecasting.
The methodologies defined here allow the system to determine when to trust social media and when to bet against it. This "meta-prediction" capability—predicting the reliability of the signal itself—is what separates a basic student project from a professional-grade quantitative analysis. The availability of high-quality, pre-processed datasets for both markets ensures that the project is feasible within the academic timeline, while the dual-branch architecture ensures it remains at the cutting edge of financial machine learning.
